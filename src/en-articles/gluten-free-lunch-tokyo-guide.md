**Gluten-free lunch in Tokyo is manageable, but it takes more care than dinner: set lunch menus are often fixed-price combos that bundle in wheat-based sides, soy sauce, or breaded dishes you can't easily substitute out.**

Lunch in Tokyo moves fast. Many restaurants run a fixed, discounted lunch set with little room to customize, which is convenient if you can eat anything and genuinely inconvenient if you can't. Dinner tends to be slower and more flexible, with more time to ask questions about a dish. Lunch rarely offers that same room, so it helps to know what to look for before you're standing at a counter with a line behind you.

## Why Gluten Hides Easily in Japanese Food

Gluten shows up in places that aren't obvious if you're not already looking for it. Regular soy sauce is brewed from wheat, and it flavors far more than just the dishes that use it directly, since it's a base ingredient in broths, dressings, and marinades. Dashi, the stock behind most Japanese soups and sauces, sometimes includes ingredients that carry gluten depending on how it's made. Fried food is another common trap: tempura batter and the panko coating on fried cutlets (tonkatsu, karaage) are wheat-based, even when the protein underneath would otherwise be fine.

A rough mental checklist for a Tokyo lunch menu:

- Rice and plain grilled or steamed protein are generally safer starting points than fried or sauced dishes.
- Soba noodles are traditionally made from buckwheat, but most Tokyo shops mix in wheat flour, so "soba" alone doesn't mean gluten-free.
- Sushi rice itself is gluten-free, but the soy sauce served alongside it almost always isn't, unless a restaurant specifically offers a gluten-free version.
- Set lunches ("teishoku") are harder to modify than à la carte dishes, since the sides are usually fixed.

## How to Order With More Confidence

A short, direct question goes further than trying to read a menu in detail: asking whether a dish contains wheat, soy sauce, or dashi, and whether the kitchen can leave any of them out, is usually enough to get a clear answer. Pointing at a specific menu item while asking tends to work better than a general question about the whole menu. If a strict gluten-free diet is a medical necessity rather than a preference, it's worth confirming directly with staff how a dish is prepared rather than assuming based on the menu description alone — the same caution that applies anywhere, not just in Tokyo.

## A Quick Pre-Lunch Checklist

- Ask about soy sauce and dashi specifically, not just "gluten" as a general term.
- Treat set lunch menus with more caution than à la carte ordering.
- Rice-based, plainly grilled dishes are usually the safest default.
- If your needs are medical rather than a preference, confirm cross-contamination directly with staff rather than assuming from the menu.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q. Is it hard to find gluten-free lunch in Tokyo?**<br>
It's manageable but takes more care than dinner, mainly because fixed-price lunch sets are harder to customize and often include wheat-based sides or soy sauce by default.

**Q. Does soy sauce contain gluten?**<br>
Regular Japanese soy sauce is brewed from wheat and does contain gluten. Gluten-free soy sauce exists but isn't standard at most restaurants unless specifically offered.

**Q. Is soba noodle gluten-free?**<br>
Not usually. While soba is traditionally made from buckwheat, most Tokyo restaurants blend in wheat flour, so it shouldn't be assumed gluten-free without asking.

**Q. Where can I get a reliable gluten-free lunch near Kitasando or Sendagaya?**<br>
Green Beans Coffee offers a one-plate gluten-free lunch with rice flour focaccia and a choice of main, though the kitchen isn't a dedicated gluten-free space.

**Q. Can restaurants in Tokyo accommodate gluten-free requests?**<br>
Many can to some degree, especially with advance notice, but it varies widely by restaurant. Asking directly about specific ingredients like soy sauce and dashi gets more reliable answers than asking generally.

**Gluten-free means avoiding gluten, a protein found in wheat, barley, and rye, usually for a medical reason or a personal dietary preference.**

Dining gluten-free in Tokyo is more manageable than a lot of visitors expect, but it helps to know what the term actually covers before trying to order around it. "Gluten-free" isn't one single thing; it ranges from a strict medical requirement to a loose lifestyle choice, and the level of caution that makes sense is different depending on which one applies to you.

## What Is Gluten, and What Does Gluten-Free Mean?

Gluten is a protein found in wheat, barley, and rye that gives bread and baked goods their elastic, chewy texture. A gluten-free diet avoids all sources of it, including obvious ones like bread, pasta, and most baked goods, and less obvious ones like soy sauce, beer, and many processed sauces that use wheat as a thickener.

## Who Actually Needs to Avoid Gluten

Three groups generally follow a gluten-free diet, for different reasons and with different levels of required strictness:

- **Celiac disease**: an autoimmune condition where gluten damages the small intestine. This requires strict, lifelong avoidance, since even small amounts of cross-contamination can trigger a reaction.
- **Non-celiac gluten sensitivity**: people who experience digestive discomfort or other symptoms from gluten without having celiac disease. Sensitivity and required strictness vary by individual.
- **Lifestyle or wellness choice**: some people reduce or eliminate gluten without a diagnosed medical reason, often alongside broader dietary changes, without needing the same strict avoidance of cross-contamination.

## Rice Flour: A Common Gluten-Free Substitute

Rice flour is one of the most common substitutes used in gluten-free baking, since rice naturally contains no gluten. It behaves differently from wheat flour, lacking the same elasticity, so gluten-free baked goods made with rice flour are typically denser or more delicate than their wheat-based counterparts, which is part of why good gluten-free bread and pastry take more care to get right.

## What to Ask About When Dining Gluten-Free

A few questions go a long way when eating out:

- Is this a dedicated gluten-free kitchen, or does it also prepare wheat-based dishes?
- Is cross-contamination a concern for your level of sensitivity?
- Are sauces, dressings, and seasonings gluten-free, since these are easy to overlook?
- Can the kitchen accommodate a gluten-free substitution if you ask in advance?

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q. What foods contain gluten?**<br>
Wheat, barley, and rye are the main sources, which means bread, pasta, most baked goods, beer, and many sauces thickened with wheat flour.

**Q. Is rice flour naturally gluten-free?**<br>
Yes. Rice contains no gluten, which makes rice flour a common substitute in gluten-free baking, though it behaves differently from wheat flour in texture.

**Q. Can people with celiac disease eat at a restaurant that isn't fully gluten-free?**<br>
It depends on the restaurant's handling of cross-contamination. Anyone with celiac disease should confirm directly with staff whether gluten-free items are prepared separately from wheat-based dishes.

**Q. Does Green Beans Coffee have gluten-free options?**<br>
Yes. A one-plate lunch with house-made rice flour focaccia and a rice flour chocolate brownie are both available, though the kitchen is not a dedicated gluten-free space.

**Q. Is gluten-free the same as low-carb?**<br>
No. Gluten-free only excludes gluten-containing grains; it doesn't restrict carbohydrates generally, and many gluten-free foods (like rice) are still high in carbs.

**Q. Do I need a medical reason to eat gluten-free?**<br>
No. Some people avoid gluten for digestive comfort or personal preference without a celiac diagnosis, though the required strictness is generally lower than for celiac disease.

## Conclusion

Gluten-free dining is really a spectrum, from strict medical necessity to a loose lifestyle choice, and knowing where you fall on it makes eating out far less stressful. In Tokyo, options like the [rice flour focaccia lunch at Green Beans Coffee](/en/articles/dog-friendly-cafe-tokyo/) show that gluten-free doesn't have to mean limited.

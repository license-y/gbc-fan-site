**Dagayasando is a local nickname, not an official place name, for the quiet, low-rise neighborhood that blends Kitasando and Sendagaya in Shibuya, Tokyo.**

You won't find Dagayasando on an official ward map, and that trips people up when they try to look it up ahead of a visit. It's a name locals use informally, the kind of thing you'd hear from someone who actually lives there rather than see printed on a train line map, and it's worth understanding before you try to plan a trip around it.

## What Is Dagayasando, Exactly?

The name is a blend of Kitasando and Sendagaya, the two station names that bracket the area, and it's used informally to describe the low-key streets between them. It's not a ward, district, or administrative boundary; it's closer to how residents of a city might casually refer to "the area around" two intersecting neighborhoods without a single clean name for it.

Officially, the area falls within Shibuya ward, in and around the Sendagaya address range, close enough to the National Olympic Stadium and Tokyo Metropolitan Gymnasium that both are walkable landmarks from here.

## What the Area Looks and Feels Like

Dagayasando doesn't have the shopping density of Shibuya or the crowds of Harajuku. What it has instead is a residential, low-rise streetscape: small design studios and independent shops sit beside ordinary apartment buildings, and the overall pace is noticeably slower than the neighborhoods on either side of it.

A few features define the character of the area:

- **No major landmarks or big-name shopping**, which keeps foot traffic low
- **A mix of residential and small commercial use**, rather than a dedicated shopping or entertainment district
- **Walkable access to real sights**, including Hatonomori Hachiman Shrine, a short walk away, and the National Olympic Stadium
- **A hidden-gem cafe culture**, including shops with no storefront signage at all

## Why It's Worth Knowing About

Most Tokyo itineraries skip this area entirely, in favor of Shinjuku, Harajuku, or Shibuya proper. That's part of what makes it worth a detour: a walk through Dagayasando feels closer to how residents actually spend an afternoon than a typical sightseeing stop does, and it's compact enough to add as a half-day addition to a longer trip rather than requiring a dedicated day.

## Dagayasando's Best-Known Hidden Spot

Green Beans Coffee is one of the area's quieter landmarks, a self-roasting specialty coffee shop with no storefront sign at all, tucked into the ground floor of an apartment building. I've covered the walk between the station, Hatonomori Hachiman Shrine, and the cafe in more detail in [a local guide to exploring Kitasando](/en/articles/discover-kitasando-tokyo/), including a suggested half-day route.

## Who This Is For

Travelers who've already covered Tokyo's bigger neighborhoods and want something quieter, as well as anyone attending an event at the National Olympic Stadium or Tokyo Metropolitan Gymnasium who wants a calm place to start or end the day nearby.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q. Is Dagayasando an official place name in Tokyo?**<br>
No. It's an informal local nickname blending Kitasando and Sendagaya, not an official ward, district, or station name.

**Q. Which ward is Dagayasando in?**<br>
The area falls within Shibuya ward, in and around the Sendagaya address range.

**Q. What's the easiest way to reach Dagayasando?**<br>
Kitasando Station and Sendagaya Station both sit at the edges of the area, each within a few minutes' walk of most of it.

**Q. Is Dagayasando close to any tourist attractions?**<br>
Yes. The National Olympic Stadium, Tokyo Metropolitan Gymnasium, and Hatonomori Hachiman Shrine are all within walking distance.

**Q. Why doesn't Dagayasando show up on typical Tokyo maps or itineraries?**<br>
Because it's an informal nickname rather than an official place name, and because the area has no major shopping or landmark draw of its own compared to neighboring Shibuya, Shinjuku, or Harajuku.

**Q. What kind of businesses are in Dagayasando?**<br>
Mostly small, independent businesses: design studios, specialty shops, and quiet cafes, several without visible storefront signage, mixed in among residential apartment buildings.

## Conclusion

Dagayasando isn't a place you'll stumble onto by checking a map; it's a name you have to already know to go looking for. Once you do, it's an easy, walkable pocket of Tokyo built around a slower pace, with [Green Beans Coffee](/en/articles/discover-kitasando-tokyo/) as one of its best reasons to visit.
